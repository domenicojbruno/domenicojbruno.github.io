I design the analog piece — the few hundred microwatts that stand between a sensor and everything digital downstream.

My focus is biopotential acquisition: EEG and ECG front-ends built in 180 nm CMOS, running deep in subthreshold where a transistor's transconductance is set by a bias current measured in nanoamperes. It is an unforgiving place to work. Gain, noise, offset and power all pull against each other, the models are least trustworthy exactly where you are operating, and nothing is fixable in software afterwards.

I care about it because of what it enables. Seizure monitoring still largely means a hospital visit and a cap of wet electrodes. Getting the same signal quality out of something you can wear all day is not a software problem — it is a circuit that has to be quiet, small and nearly free to run. That constraint is the interesting part.

Right now I am a research assistant in WPI's Integrated Circuits and Systems Lab working on wearable and physiological sensing, and a teaching assistant for Microelectronics I. I co-founded WPI's chapter of the IEEE Solid-State Circuits Society because there was no room on campus where people talked about this work, and now there is.

This past summer I was at MIT Lincoln Laboratory, on the RF transmitter team at the Lincoln Space Surveillance Complex — building hardware to replace obsolete equipment on the Millstone Hill Radar, which has been running and evolving since 1957. My favourite part was that the work is never finished: every upgrade is the starting point for the next one. Schematics, boards, chassis, and a lot of time on a spectrum analyser finding out how the thing I designed actually behaves. That gap, between the simulation and the bench, is the part of engineering I want more of.
